Your AI App's Moat Isn't the Model

I work on web-access infrastructure, so I'll admit I have a stake in this. But it's also why I keep noticing the same thing: most AI products treat the model as their advantage, and I think that's backwards. The durable moat is web access. Here's why I've come around to that.

Every AI app is one string away from its competitor

Open the code behind most AI products and somewhere there's a line that names a model: some claude-... or gpt-... identifier. Change that string, and you're running on a different company's model. It's close to a one-line diff.

Sit with that for a second, because it's the whole problem. If your product is a wrapper around a model call, then everything that makes it work is a call your competitor can make too. Same models, same API, same docs, same pricing tiers. The prompt that feels like your secret sauce is copyable in an afternoon by anyone who can read your output and guess backward.

That's not a moat. That's a head start measured in weeks.

I don't think most people building AI products have fully priced this in. The mental model is still "we have a great model and clever prompting." But the models are converging: the gap between frontier providers narrows with every release, and they're rented, not owned. You are building on top of infrastructure that your competitors rent from the same landlords on the same terms.

So if the model isn't the moat, what is?

The model reasons. It doesn't know anything fresh.

Here's the thing a model can't do on its own: tell you something that happened after it was trained. It can't tell you what a product costs right now, what a page says today, or whether a fact you're relying on changed this morning. For anything that lives in the present, the model is blind, and it needs something to go get the information for it.

The intelligence is in the box on the left. The knowledge comes from everything to the right of it.

And everything to the right of it is hard in a way the model is not. The model you can rent. The pipeline to reality you have to build.

Why access is a real moat, and the model isn't

Three things make web access defensible in a way that a model wrapper never will be.

It compounds instead of resetting. Every new model generation resets the capability race: whatever edge you had from being on the best model evaporates the moment your competitor swaps their string to the same one. Web-access infrastructure doesn't reset like that. The work you did last quarter to reliably pull data from a hard category of sites is still working this quarter, on top of whatever model came out in between. It accumulates. Model advantage evaporates; access advantage stacks.

It's specific, so it can't be bought off a shelf. There's no single API you can buy that hands you reliable, structured, current data across every domain your product touches. Getting clean data out of one category of site is its own problem, with its own failure modes, and the next category is a different problem again. That specificity is exactly what makes it a moat: a competitor can't purchase their way past it, they have to build it, site by messy site.

This is also why "we'll just use a search API" isn't the answer people think it is. Search tells you where something is. It doesn't get you the fresh, structured version of what's actually there right now, and for anything that changes, that gap is the entire problem.

It's a moat that fights back. This is the strongest property and the most underrated. Web access degrades without maintenance. Sites change, block, restructure, move behind new defenses. That sounds like a bug: it's the feature. A moat that requires constant upkeep to hold is a moat your competitor also has to keep up, continuously, forever. It's not a wall you build once; it's a position you hold. Those are the hardest advantages to take from you, because catching up isn't a one-time effort. It's a permanent tax.

There's no permanent solution to buy, which is exactly why the people who've been holding the position have something the newcomer can't instantly acquire.

The strongest argument against me

I don't want to pretend this wins cleanly, because it doesn't, and the honest counterargument is worth more than another paragraph of my side.

Models aren't nothing. A large enough jump in capability can eat an entire category of infrastructure overnight. Better reasoning can mean needing less retrieval: a model that's right more often needs to look things up less often. If some future model has current-enough knowledge baked in, or can navigate the web natively without a retrieval layer at all, then a chunk of what I've called a moat becomes a feature the model provider shipped for free. It's happened before in this field: today's painstakingly-built capability becomes tomorrow's default API parameter.

So this isn't "models don't matter." Models matter enormously. The claim is narrower and I want to be precise about it: models matter as capability, but they're converging as advantage. What you can do with the best model, your competitor can do next month with the same one. The question isn't whether the model is powerful. It's whether the model is yours in any way that lasts. It usually isn't.

What actually lasts

Strip a mature AI product down and ask what a competitor genuinely can't replicate in a week.

Not the model: they rent the same one. Not the prompt: they can reverse-engineer it. Not the UI: they can clone it. What's left is the unglamorous machinery that reliably gets the right information out of a world that doesn't hand it over cheaply: the retrieval, the extraction, the freshness, the accumulated, maintained, per-domain knowledge of how to actually get the data.

That's the part that took months. That's the part that's still working after the model changed. That's the moat.

The AI products that survive the next few years won't be the ones with the cleverest prompts or even the best models, because those converge to the same place. They'll be the ones that solved the boring problem underneath: getting reality into the model, freshly and reliably, at scale.

Intelligence is commoditizing. Access to reality isn't.

To restate the bias so you don't have to scroll up: this is what I work on. The argument's the part that matters.
